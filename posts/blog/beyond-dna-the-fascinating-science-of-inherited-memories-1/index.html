Beyond DNA: The Fascinating Science of Inherited Memories

USER: SYS_ADMIN | DATE: 2026-06-24 | PID: Transgenerational-Epigenetic-Inheritance

An Unexpected Breakthrough

I got to know of this topic by a youtube video which showed how an 8 year-old was able to showcase that butterflies were able to retain their memories through metamorphosis (the process of becoming butterfly from a chrysalis) and within generations.

The fact that a young kid was able to come to this conclusion on his own and the fact that this means that we are all in a sense retaining memories of our ancestors made it all very fascinating for me.

Which led me to dive deeper into this topic to understand what it is and how much we know about it. This post is basically a log of my findings curated in a digestible manner.

Story that Shook the World of Entomology

The story starts with a 8-10 year old Japanese student named Jo Nagai who raised swallowtail butterflies. He trained the caterpillars to hate the smell of lavender oil using mild shocks from a muscle therapy device. Which involved exposing the caterpillars to lavender smell and then giving them light shock (correlating the smell of lavender with an electric shock).

Then his experiment (a summer break project) was supposed to be waiting for the caterpillars to grow into butterflies, then put them in a Y-shaped tube where a control group (a group which was not shocked and grew naturally without the knowledge of lavender oil smell).

Jo Nagai then proceeded to put both the control group (Group A) and the group with lavender oil association (Group B) on the same end of the Y tube where both the ends of Y would contain sweet water that butterflies would be naturally gravitated towards. With a twist, one end contained a cotton ball which was soaked in lavender oil and the other didn’t. This was to measure if the smell has any effect of the decision of the butterflies.

As expected Group A (control group) led to an almost 50-50 split on choosing one end over the other end. While, Group B (lavender oil smell association with electric shock group) choose the non-lavender oil tunnel over the one which had lavender oil smell.

This proved that even butterflies (previously proven for moths) are able to retain memories after going through metamorphosis. Which was a research paper worthy outcome.

Why Stop Here?

After this discovery, Jo Nagai didn’t stop there. He pondered, and thought he himself had some allergies that his parents did. But his friends didn’t have those allergies, which led him to the thought of what about the offsprings of Group B?

Will they retain this association with the smell? Or will they forget and behave the same as Group A?

The only way to get the answer to these questions was to test it out, which is what Jo Nagai decided to do.

Jo Nagai’s methodology was as rigorous as it was compassionate. He trained a “Parent Generation” of caterpillars to associate the scent of lavender with a mild discomfort a level-four electric shock he first tested on himself to ensure it wasn’t cruel.

After testing the same experiment on the child generations and the grandchild generation it led to the shocking result of them avoiding the scent even through they were never trained for the smell and shock association.

A Scientific Revolution: Epigenetic Inheritance

The fact that the second and third generations avoided the scent without any training suggests transgenerational epigenetic inheritance. This means the environment and experiences of the parents caused a biological “tag” to be placed on their DNA, which was then passed to their offspring.

While this concept has been explored in mice and tiny worms, finding it in complex insects like Swallowtail butterflies especially across the complete cellular reconstruction of metamorphosis is groundbreaking. Jo even observed that the inheritance seemed stronger in male offspring, hinting at a sex-linked genetic pattern that could redefine our understanding of insect biology.

But this leads to the following question.

How Does a Memory Actually Pass Down?

To understand why this discovery is so mind-bending, we have to look at how we used to think inheritance worked. For a long time, the rule in biology was simple: you inherit your parents’ DNA, and that DNA is a fixed, unchanging set of instructions.

Think of DNA like a massive, printed encyclopedia. The letters on the pages are fixed in ink. If your parents learned to fear a specific smell, or learned to ride a bike, or went through a stressful event, that was simply an experience processed in their brain. It didn’t literally rewrite the printed text of their genetic code.

So, if the DNA code itself isn’t changing, how did Jo Nagai’s butterflies pass down the fear of lavender? How do any inherited memories work?

The answer lies in a secondary layer of information called the Epigenome (which literally translates to “above the genome”).

The Highlighters and Sticky Notes of Life

If DNA is the printed text in the encyclopedia, the epigenome is a collection of sticky notes, paperclips, and neon highlighters added by the reader.

When a living being goes through a significant, intense experience, like the trauma of an electric shock or even a period of great joy, the body reacts by releasing a flood of chemicals. Some of these chemicals act as “tags” (often called methyl groups) that physically attach themselves to the outside of the DNA.

These tags don’t change the actual words on the page. Instead, they act like volume knobs. They tell the body, “Hey, read this specific section of the DNA much louder,” or “Ignore this section completely.”

The Missing Bridge: From Brain to Offspring

There is still one massive missing piece to the puzzle. If a butterfly (or a mouse, or a human) gets stressed and creates these chemical tags in its brain, how do those tags end up in the reproductive cells to be passed on to the children?

For decades, scientists thought the brain and the reproductive system were completely sealed off from each other. A memory was strictly local to the brain. But recent breakthroughs have shown that the body has a hidden messaging system.

When a brain experiences a severe stressor—like a shock paired with a specific smell. The active brain cells release tiny chemical messengers known as small RNAs. These messengers act like specific warning letters. They travel out of the brain, move through the bloodstream, and enter the reproductive cells (sperm or eggs). Once there, they attach those epigenetic “sticky notes” directly onto the DNA that will eventually become the offspring.

In the case of our lavender-fearing butterflies, the parents essentially put a massive neon highlighter over the genetic instructions for lavender smell receptors and added a sticky note that said: Danger, pay close attention to this.

When the next generation is born, their base DNA is identical to their parents. But because of those highlighters, they are born with a hyper-sensitivity to that specific smell. They don’t inherit a conscious “thought” of being shocked; they just inherit an overwhelming, instinctual alarm bell that rings whenever that scent is near.

Inheriting the Threat: When the World is Dangerous

To really understand how these epigenetic “sticky notes” change behavior, we have to look at what happens when the environment is hostile. Why would nature want an offspring to inherit a memory of pain it never experienced?

The most famous breakthrough on this came from a 2014 study on mice at Emory University. Scientists took male mice and exposed them to the scent of cherry blossoms while giving them a mild shock. Eventually, the mice learned to fear the smell.

When those mice had children (and later, grandchildren), the researchers exposed the offspring to the cherry blossom scent. Even though these new generations had never been shocked, and had never even met their fathers, they exhibited a severe panic response the moment they smelled cherry blossoms.

But here is the fascinating part: the offspring didn’t inherit a complex, newly built brain pathway that logically connected “cherry blossom” to “pain.”

Instead, the epigenetic tags from the father caused the offspring to be born with physically larger, more abundant smell receptors specifically tuned to the cherry blossom scent. To the offspring, a normal whiff of that scent was amplified into a deafening, overwhelming signal. In the mammalian brain, a sudden, massive sensory overload automatically triggers the fear center (the amygdala).

Nature didn’t pass down a specific thought; it passed down an incredibly sensitive alarm system. If the parent’s environment was full of a specific danger, the offspring is born with an amplified sensor for that exact threat, ensuring they hide before they ever have to learn the hard way.

What About the Good Stuff? Inheriting Joy

This brings up an incredible question. If trauma, fear, and pain can place highlighters on our DNA and pass down to our children, what about happiness? What happens if a parent lives a highly enriching, fulfilling life?

For a long time, evolutionary biology focused heavily on survival and fear. But recent studies into what scientists call “Environmental Enrichment” have proven that positive traits are absolutely inherited too.

When an animal is placed in an enriched environment one with plenty of exercise, complex puzzles to solve, and a rich social life their brain is flooded with “feel-good” chemicals like dopamine, and a crucial protein responsible for learning and memory called BDNF.

Just like with the trauma response, this sustained state of thriving generates its own chemical messengers that travel to the reproductive system. But instead of tagging the DNA to build an amplified alarm sensor, they tag the DNA to upgrade the brain’s receivers for reward and learning.

When the offspring of these enriched parents are born, they inherit a vastly upgraded baseline for happiness and resilience. Research shows these children naturally produce more BDNF and have a higher density of dopamine receptors.

What does this mean in plain English? It means it takes less effort for them to feel satisfied. They learn faster, their memories are sharper, and when they do encounter a stressful situation, their brains recover much quicker. They are biologically pre-wired to be optimistic explorers rather than anxious hiders.

The Evolutionary Gamble

If inheriting an optimized, “happy” brain makes an animal learn faster and bounce back from stress quicker, why didn’t evolution just permanently hardwire all of us that way? Why does the fear-inheritance system even exist?

Because in the brutal reality of nature, epigenetics is a calculated gamble on the environment.

Evolution doesn’t care about happiness; it cares about survival. If a parent is living in an environment saturated with predators, famine, or extreme violence, an optimistic, highly exploratory offspring is going to get eaten on day one. In a hostile world, a paranoid, high-anxiety, hide-in-the-shadows instinct is the only way to survive long enough to have children of your own.

Think of these epigenetic tags as a biological weather forecast passed from parent to child. The parent’s body is saying: “Here is the world I lived in. Boot up with the hardware that will keep you alive in this specific environment.”

If the parent signals that the world is safe and rich in resources, the offspring gets the “explore and thrive” firmware. If the parent signals constant danger, the offspring gets the “hide and survive” firmware. It is an incredibly elegant, hot-swappable survival system.

The Takeaway: You Hold the Pen

When people first hear about generational trauma and epigenetic inheritance, it can feel incredibly heavy. It sounds like we are permanently doomed to carry the anxieties, fears, and traumas of our parents and grandparents

But that is the beauty of the epigenome: it is written in pencil, not ink.

Unlike your base DNA, which you are stuck with for life, epigenetic tags are highly volatile. They are designed to be overwritten if the environment changes. Scientists call this Epigenetic Drift.

If an offspring inherits a massive fear response but grows up in a safe, loving, and predictable environment, their brain eventually realizes that the inherited “alarm system” is a waste of energy. Over time sometimes within a single lifetime, and almost always within three to four generations—the body naturally physically strips those sticky notes off the DNA.

Even better, we can actively rewrite our own tags. When humans go through exposure therapy, build strong social networks, exercise, or actively manage their stress, they aren’t just “feeling better.” They are physically generating new chemical signals that recruit enzymes (like TET proteins) to act as molecular erasers, wiping away the trauma tags and replacing them with resilience tags.

You are not just a passive recipient of your ancestors’ memories. Your brain is constantly generating new data based on how you live right now. By actively working to find joy, manage your stress, and enrich your own life, you are doing more than healing yourself. You are literally rewriting the biological configuration file of yourself and potentially the ones to come.
